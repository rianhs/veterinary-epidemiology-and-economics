Correct an apparent prevalence

A blood test is positive for 25 of 200 cattle. How many of the 200 cattle are truly infected?

The scenario: brucellosis testing in one village

A district animal health officer takes a blood sample from each of the 200 dairy cattle in one village.

The laboratory tests every sample for brucellosis. The test is positive for 25 cattle and negative for 175 cattle.

The officer must report how many of the 200 cattle are infected.

The test is positive for 25 of 200 cattle, or 12.5%. What share of the 200 cattle is truly infected?

The test misses some infected cattle, and it is positive for some cattle without infection. Choose your prediction.

Apparent prevalence

The apparent prevalence is the share of tested animals with a positive test result.

Infection and a positive test result

In the village, two groups of cattle matter: the cattle that carry the infection, and the cattle with a positive test.

The infected cattle form one group. The cattle with a positive test form the other group.

The two groups overlap only in part. The cattle outside the overlap have a wrong test result.

Four kinds of test result

Each of the 200 cattle has an infection status and a test result. Together they give four kinds of test result.

True positive

Infected, and the test is positive.

16 cattle

False negative

Infected, but the test is negative.

4 cattle

False positive

Not infected, but the test is positive.

9 cattle

True negative

Not infected, and the test is negative.

171 cattle

A false result is a test result that disagrees with the infection status of the animal.

The 200 cattle in one grid

Each square in the grid is one of the 200 cattle. The colour and the outline show the kind of test result.

  • True positive: 16 cattle
  • False positive: 9 cattle
  • False negative: 4 cattle
  • True negative: 171 cattle

The test reports the 16 true positives and the 9 false positives, which is 25 positive results.

Apparent vs true prevalence

The same 200 cattle give two different prevalences. One counts positive test results, and the other counts infected cattle.

Apparent prevalence

Counts: cattle with a positive test

In the village: 25 of 200 cattle, or 12.5%

Includes: the 9 false positives, and it misses the 4 false negatives

True prevalence

Counts: cattle that are infected

In the village: 20 of 200 cattle, or 10.0%

Includes: the 16 true positives and the 4 false negatives

The apparent prevalence is higher than the true prevalence here, because the 9 false positives are more than the 4 false negatives.

Test sensitivity and test specificity

Two numbers describe how well the blood test works. Each number is a share of one row of the table.

Test sensitivity (Se)

The share of infected cattle that test positive.

Test specificity (Sp)

The share of cattle without infection that test negative.

NumberRow of the tableCountValue
Test sensitivityInfected cattle that test positive16 of 200.80
Test specificityCattle without infection that test negative171 of 1800.95

The laboratory gives Se and Sp for each test. The officer can then correct the apparent prevalence without knowing which cattle are infected.

Worked example: the correction for the village

The officer knows the apparent prevalence and the Se and Sp of the test. Three steps give the true prevalence.

StepWhat the officer doesResult
1Take the apparent prevalence: 25 of 200 cattle0.125
2Add Sp and subtract 1: 0.125 + 0.95 − 10.075
3Divide by Se + Sp − 1: 0.075 / (0.80 + 0.95 − 1) = 0.075 / 0.750.10

The true prevalence is 0.10, so about 20 of the 200 cattle are infected. This matches the row "Infected" of the table.

A new scenario: goats at a livestock market

A veterinarian tests 400 goats at a market. The test is positive for 40 goats. The test has Se 0.90 and Sp 0.98.

Which statement about the 40 positive goats is correct?

Key points

  • The apparent prevalence counts positive test results. The true prevalence counts infected animals.
  • False positives push the apparent prevalence up. False negatives pull it down.
  • With a known Se and Sp, you can estimate the true prevalence.
  • The correction fails when the apparent prevalence is below 1 − Sp.
