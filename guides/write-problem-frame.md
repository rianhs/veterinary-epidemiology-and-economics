## Purpose

This guide shows you how to write a problem frame for a population-level health problem. A [problem frame](../glossary.md) is a short written statement with five parts: population, outcome, decision, decision maker, and objective. The frame uses at most three sentences. You are done when the frame has three sentences or fewer, each of the five parts appears once, and the decision maker confirms it.

## When to use this procedure

Use this procedure when a request about a health problem in a population is wide or unclear. An example is "tell me everything about this disease". Write the frame before you look for information or choose an analysis.

Do not use this procedure for the care of one animal. Clinical work does not need a frame. When the frame is ready and you need to list the information that the decision requires, use [List the information that a decision needs](list-information-for-decision.md).

## Procedure

The running example is a layer farm with 8,000 hens in 8 pens. The farm lost 120 hens in one week, mostly in pens A1 and B1. The laboratory found Salmonella, and the farm had no earlier positive result. The owner asks a technical service consultant for everything about Salmonella. These numbers are synthetic and are for teaching.

### Step 1. Place the problem in the five questions

Write the request of the client in one sentence. List which of the five questions the client needs answered. Mark the question about what should be done, because a frame always supports a decision.

Check: You can name the main question and the earlier questions that feed it.

Example: The owner needs to know what to do, so the main question is what should be done. The laboratory has partly answered what is happening.

### Step 2. Name the decision maker and the decision

Warning: The consultant and the laboratory give advice and information. They do not choose between actions.

Find the person or body that chooses between actions and pays for them. Write the choice as "whether to do A or B". Use a real choice between two or more actions.

Check: The decision names at least two actions, and the decision maker can choose between them.

Example: The decision maker is the farm owner. The decision is whether to clean pens A1 and B1 only or all 8 pens.

### Step 3. Choose the level and name the population

Choose the level of analysis at which the decision acts. Name the defined group of animals at that level. Give its size and its place.

Check: The population is the whole group that the decision covers, with a size and a place.

Example: The decision acts on the farm, so the level is the animal population. The population is the 8,000 hens in the 8 pens of the farm.

### Step 4. Name the outcome

Write the health event or consequence that the decision should change. Choose terms that a reader can count in the population.

Check: A reader can count the outcome in the population.

Example: The outcome is deaths and Salmonella-positive hens.

### Step 5. Find the population status and set the objective

Find the population status of the disease in the population. Read the typical objective for that status in the article on population status. Write the objective in one phrase.

If the status is not known, write "status not known" and set the objective to find the status.

Check: The objective is to reduce the problem or to keep it out, in the form that fits the status.

Example: Cases appeared in one week in two pens, and the farm had no earlier positive result. The status is close to an outbreak. The objective is to contain the problem and find its source.

### Step 6. Write the frame in at most three sentences

Write the population and the outcome in the first sentence. Write the decision maker and the decision in the second sentence. Write the objective in the third sentence.

Check: The frame has three sentences or fewer, and each of the five parts appears once.

Example: In the 8,000 hens of the farm, deaths and Salmonella-positive hens have occurred. The farm owner must decide whether to clean pens A1 and B1 only or all 8 pens. The objective is to contain the problem and find its source.

### Step 7. Test the frame with the decision maker

Show the frame to the decision maker. Ask whether it describes the problem that needs a decision. Change each part that the decision maker corrects.

Check: The decision maker confirms all five parts.

Example: The owner confirms the frame. The owner also asks what to do about the eggs that reached two markets. This is another decision, so you write a second frame for it.

## Interpreting the results

The frame tells you which information you need next. Each part leads to questions that information can answer, and the next guide shows how to list them.

If a part cannot be written, then information is missing. Ask the client for it before you continue.

If the client names several decisions, then write one frame for each decision. A single frame with several decisions cannot guide the search for information.

If two decision makers share one problem, then write one frame for each of them. The population, the decision, and the objective can differ between decision makers.

If the status is not known, then the objective is to find the status. A lack of reports does not show that the disease is absent, because weak surveillance also produces silence.

If the frame needs more than three sentences, then the problem is probably two problems. Split it and write one frame for each.

## Common mistakes

The first mistake is to name the consultant or the laboratory as the decision maker. These roles give advice and information, and they do not choose between actions. Ask who decides and who pays, and name that person.

The second mistake is to write a question for information in place of a decision, for example "Is Salmonella present in the flock?". This question asks what is happening, and it does not offer a choice between actions. Move the question to the list of information, and write the choice that the answer will support.

The third mistake is to use a sample or a product as the population. Five tested hens are a sample, and eggs are a product of the system. The population is the whole defined group of animals that the decision covers.

The fourth mistake is to set the objective before the status is known. An objective of elimination does not fit an outbreak that has just started. An objective of keeping the disease out does not fit a disease that is already present. Find the status first, and then read the objective that fits it.
