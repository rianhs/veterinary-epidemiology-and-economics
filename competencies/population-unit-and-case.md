## S2.1 Identify the epidemiological unit {#s2-1}

```{=html}
<div class="skill-body"><p class="skill-scope">A group of animals that share about the same likelihood of exposure to a hazard, such as a pen, herd, flock, or village (WOAH). A hazard is a biological, chemical, or physical agent, for example a virus, a toxin, or a contaminated batch of feed. The unit depends on the hazard and on how the hazard reaches the animals. Distance, shared surroundings, and shared management join animals into one unit or separate them into two units. The epidemiological, observation, and analysis units: choose each for a problem and recognize when the three differ. Animal-level and herd-level shares of the same findings.</p>
<div class="skill-grid"><div><h3 class="mini-title">Depth</h3><p class="depth depth-core">Core</p></div>
```

## S2.2 Distinguish the populations of a survey {#s2-2}

```{=html}
<div class="skill-body"><p class="skill-scope">Target population: the animals that the answer should describe. Source population: the part of the target population that the team can reach, for example through lists. Study population: the units that the team actually includes. The three are nested. How much of the target population the answer describes, and what each step leaves out.</p>
```

## S2.5 Choose a method to estimate population size {#s2-5}

```{=html}
<div class="skill-body"><p class="skill-scope">Stock and flow: births, deaths, sales, purchases. Structure of a population by age, sex, and density. What the choice depends on: the purpose of the count, who is counted (owned, semi-owned, or unowned animals), the biology and behaviour of the animals, scale in space and time, resources, and ethics. Methods that collect new data: census, area sample, quadrat and transect, capture-mark-recapture, signs of presence, and reports by people. Methods that reuse data: administrative records, earlier studies, and derived estimates. Triangulation, assumptions, and uncertainty of an estimate.</p>
```
