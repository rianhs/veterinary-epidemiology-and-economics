Parts of the definition of epidemiology

Epidemiology is the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified populations, and the application of this study to the control of health problems.

Part of the definitionWhat it asks the epidemiologist to do
ObjectStudy health-related states or events in specified populations.
Work 1: DistributionDescribe how often the events occur, by animal, place, and time.
Work 2: DeterminantsExplain which factors change how often the events occur.
Work 3: ApplicationUse the results to control health problems and to check the measures.

Descriptive vs analytical epidemiology

Descriptive epidemiology describes the cases, whereas analytical epidemiology compares cases with non-cases. Here, the cases are the 120 hens that died, and the non-cases are the 7,880 hens that did not die.

The population-to-intervention loop

The population-to-intervention loop has six boxes. It turns what we see into what we do. Each box is what we hold at one moment. After box 6, the loop goes back to box 1, because the intervention changes the population.

Trace the loop with a finger: boxes 1 to 6, then back to box 1. Stress that box 1 is the same population at a later time, so the loop has six boxes. Open the details for the meaning of each box.

Second turn of the population-to-intervention loop

The measure changes the population. A new observation then shows the changed population. Comparing it with the first observation answers the question: did it work? Without the first observation, there is nothing to compare.

D5 is the comparison between two turns of the loop. No box holds it. Ask what the farm would lack if nobody had counted the deaths before the measure.

Find the error

The laboratory diagnostician writes a note.

  1. The laboratory counts the dead hens by pen: 90 of the 120 are in pens A1 and B1.
  2. The laboratory plans to compare the share of hens that died in pens A1 and B1 with the share in the other six pens.
  3. Pens A1 and B1 are next to the water inlet, so the water caused the deaths.

Which sentence draws a conclusion that its data cannot support?

For each sentence ask which question it answers. Sentence 1 answers what is happening. Sentence 2 is a plan to answer why, and it compares two groups. Sentence 3 answers why, but it has only the count. A count shows where the deaths are. It does not show their cause.

The owner repairs the water line. The laboratory tests the flock again and compares the result with the first test. Which question does this comparison answer?

Which statement is correct?

Key points

  • The definition of epidemiology has an object and three kinds of work: (1) distribution, (2) determinants, and (3) application.
  • Descriptive epidemiology describes the cases: how many, which animals, where, and when. Analytical epidemiology compares cases with non-cases to look for determinants.
  • The course has five questions, one for each domain: what is happening (Domain 1), why is it happening (Domain 2), what could happen (Domain 3), what should be done (Domain 4), and did it work (Domain 5).
  • The population-to-intervention loop has six boxes. The arrows are the work between the boxes. After box 6 the loop returns to box 1, and Domain 5 compares two turns.
  • An answer needs the work of its own question. A count of cases does not explain why.
