C1 Epidemiological and economic thinking

Frame a problem as an answerable question

After 120 hens died on a layer farm in one week, a consultant has eight questions in mind. Which of these questions can be answered with evidence, and what should the consultant do with the remaining questions?

  • S1.6Frame a problem as an answerable question
  • TimeAbout 20 minutes
  • BeforeExplain epidemiological approaches and questions
Read the lead aloud. Ask for a first answer in one sentence. The deck gives the course answer: four checks that sort a question, and a way to list the information that an answerable question needs.

The case: Salmonella on a layer farm

In one week, 120 of the 8,000 hens on a layer farm die. Most of the dead hens come from pens A1 and B1, which are located near the water inlet.

The laboratory tests five dead hens and finds Salmonella in all five. Eggs from pens A1 and B1 have reached two markets.

You are the technical service consultant of a poultry company. The farm owner phones you and says that the farm has IDR 15,000,000 left to spend this month. On the way to the farm, you write down the questions that come to your mind. Some come from the owner. Some are your own.

The answer key: three questions for epidemiology or economics (counts by pen, the comparison of pens, the choice between measures), three for other disciplines (microbiology, clinical medicine, law), and two that nobody can answer as asked (no time frame, and a word without a criterion).

Answerable question

An answerable question is a question that someone can answer with evidence, and its answer can change what someone does.

ExampleWhat would finish the work?
Request“Tell me everything about Salmonella.”Nothing. Another fact always exists.
Answerable question“How many hens died in each pen, and on which days?”A table of the deaths by pen and by day.

There is a difference between a request and a question. A request names a topic, and a topic has no end, so nobody can finish it. A question has an end, because evidence can answer it. The answer of a question also has a use: it helps someone to decide what to do.

The first task of the consultant is to turn a request into questions.

Ask the class what the consultant would hand over to finish the request on the first row. There is no such thing. Then ask the same for the second row.

The four checks of a question

A check is one thing to confirm about a question before you try to answer it. An answerable question passes all four checks, in this order.

CheckAskIf the question fails
1 ClearDoes every word have a meaning that someone can measure, and is everything that the question assumes true?Rewrite the question with defined words and remove the assumption.
2 EvidenceCould an observation show that one answer is right and another is wrong?Add a time frame. Replace “ever” or “never” with a chance.
3 DisciplineIs the answer a fact about animals in a population or in a chain, about causes, about what could happen, or about what options give?Give the question to the discipline that answers it. Keep its answer as possible data.
4 ChoiceWould a different answer lead someone to a different action?Drop the question, or find the choice that it serves.
Say the order out loud. A question with no clear meaning cannot be tested for evidence, so check 1 comes first. Check 3 and check 4 only make sense for a question that has an answer.

Checks 1 and 2: a question that has an answer

CheckQuestionWhy it failsA better question
1 Clear“Is this farm healthy?”“Healthy” has no criterion, so nobody can say which result means healthy.Did more hens die this week than the expected level for this flock?
1 Clear“Which feed batch brought Salmonella to the farm?”It assumes that the feed is the source of Salmonella. The case does not show this.Based on the available data, what could be the actual source (feed, water, new poultry, or humans)?
2 Evidence“Will this ever happen on this farm again?”It has no time frame. No observation can show that it will never happen.What is the chance that the next flock is infected with Salmonella during its time on the farm, if nothing changes?

A question that fails check 1 or check 2 cannot be answered in its current form, so rewrite it. A term needs a criterion that someone can measure, an assumption needs evidence, and “ever” needs a time frame.

The better question for the second row is open on purpose: it lets the data speak about the source. Ask which of the three better questions belongs to which of the five questions of the course. The answers are Domain 1, Domain 2, and Domain 3. Slide 9 gives the types.

Check 3: the discipline that answers the question

Check 3 asks which discipline answers the question. Epidemiology and economics answer questions about animals in a population or in a chain. The other questions need another kind of answer, from another discipline.

These are the same six questions as the first six cards on slide 3, minus the two that nobody can answer. Ask which cards students placed differently. An answer from another discipline can become data for epidemiology: the details panel gives an example.

Check 4: the choice that the answer serves

A question is worth asking when its possible answers lead to different actions. Describe the choice of the owner with three things.

Decision maker

The person who chooses. The owner chooses for the farm.

Options

The actions that the decision maker can choose between. There are at least two. The owner can choose measure A (test the eggs), B (repair the water line), or C (vaccinate the next flock). Each option has an opportunity cost.

Objective

What the choice should achieve. The disease status of the population gives the objective. The farm has an outbreak, so the objective is to reduce the disease.

QuestionDoes a different answer change the choice?
Is the water line the source of the infection?Yes. If it is the source, measure B is worth more. If it is not, the owner may choose measure A or C.
On which hour of the day did the first hen die?No. The owner chooses the same measure, whatever the hour.

A question that fails check 4 can have an answer and still not be worth the time. Drop it, or find the choice that it serves.

The three parts come from the earlier decks: the opportunity cost of the options, and the objective that follows from the disease status. Ask students what the objective would be on a farm where nobody has tested the hens.

The five questions as types of question

A question that passes the four checks has one of five types. The five types are the five questions of the course. Each type needs certain parts in its sentence. Every type needs a population and an outcome.

DomainThe question of the courseParts that the sentence needsExample
Domain 1What is happening?Population, health event, place, timeHow many hens have died, in which pens, and on which days?
Domain 2Why is it happening?Population, outcome, exposure, and a group to compare withWhy did more hens die in pens A1 and B1 than in the other six pens?
Domain 3What could happen?Population, outcome, a scenario, and a periodWhat is the chance that the next flock is infected during its time on the farm, if nothing changes?
Domain 4What should be done?The options, and what each gives in health and in moneyWhich measure gives the owner the most benefit for IDR 15,000,000?
Domain 5Did it work?The measure, a comparison (before, or without it), and the outcomeDid fewer hens die per week after the owner repaired the water line?

Keep or drop a smaller question

Check 4 asked whether a different answer would change the choice of the owner. Apply the same test to each smaller question: keep it only if a different answer could lead the owner to a different measure.

The figure shows a question that stays. A second question goes: “On which hour of the day did the first hen die?” Whatever the hour is, the owner chooses the same measure, so the answer helps no choice.

The IDR 45 million is the estimate of the earlier deck on veterinary economics. Ask students to name another smaller question that fails the test, for example the name of the technician who took the samples.

Sources of information

Every smaller question needs a source, the place where its answer comes from. Three kinds of source exist.

Existing records

Farm records, laboratory results, and surveillance reports of the authority.

New data

A visit, samples, or questions to people.

Published knowledge and experts

Studies, textbooks, guidelines, and specialists.

When a source is usable

A source is usable when the answer arrives in time, at a cost that the decision maker accepts, and with a quality that is good enough for the question. When no source is usable, change the question, or state an assumption and say that it is an assumption.

Example: the owner cannot wait for water samples, so the consultant assumes that the water line is the source, and says so.

The path of a question

The return arrow is the key point. A question that nobody can supply with information is not yet an answerable question. Ask students for a smaller question in the owner's case that has no usable source today.

Worked example: the problem frame of the farm owner

A problem frame has four parts: the choice, the objective, the main question, and the information that the question needs. This slide shows the first three parts.

Choice
The farm owner is the decision maker and chooses one measure. A: test the eggs from the two pens for Salmonella. B: repair the water line and disinfect the two pens. C: vaccinate the next flock of hens against Salmonella. Each costs IDR 15 million.
Objective
Reduce the disease. The farm has an outbreak: 120 hens died in one week, and the expected level is about 10.
Main question
Which measure gives the owner the most benefit for IDR 15,000,000? This is a Domain 4 question.
  • Check 1, clear. “Benefit” means the loss that a measure avoids, in rupiah.
  • Check 2, evidence. Records, tests, and estimates can show which measure is worth more.
  • Check 3, discipline. It asks what options give and cost: epidemiology and economics.
  • Check 4, choice. The owner picks one measure, and different answers can lead to a different measure.
The three measures, the budget, and the avoided losses come from the deck on veterinary economics. The expected level of about 10 deaths per week comes from the deck on disease status. Slide 14 lists the information that this question needs.

A new case: a tilapia farm

A production animal veterinarian visits a tilapia farm with 20 ponds. Last week, fish died in 3 ponds. Nobody has tested the fish, and nobody has looked at the other 17 ponds. The owner asks what to do.

Which objective and which first question fit this situation?

Key points

  • An answerable question is a question that someone can answer with evidence, and whose answer can change what someone does. A request names a topic, and a topic has no end.
  • Four checks sort a question, in this order: are the words clear, can evidence answer it, does epidemiology or economics answer it, and does a different answer change a choice. If a question fails check 1 or 2, rewrite it. If it fails check 3, give it to another discipline. If it fails check 4, drop it.
  • A question that passes the four checks has one of five types. The type tells which parts the sentence needs.
  • A choice has a decision maker, options, and an objective. Different decision makers look at different populations, so their problem frames differ.
  • The main question needs information. Write each piece of information as a smaller question. Keep a smaller question only if a different answer could change the choice. Then name a usable source: existing records, new data, or published knowledge and experts.
