Epidemiology is the study of the distribution and determinants of health-related states or events in specified populations, and the application of this study to the control of health problems. In veterinary epidemiology, the specified populations are groups of animals, such as herds, flocks, and village populations, together with the animal products that come from them.

## Background

A veterinarian learns to ask a clinical question about one animal. A hen is lethargic and has stopped laying, so the veterinarian asks what is wrong with her and which treatment she needs. The answer comes from the history, the examination, and tests on that animal.

Some problems cannot be solved in this way. A farm owner reports that 40 hens died this week in one layer house. Treating the dead hens is not possible. The useful questions are about the whole flock: how many hens are affected, which pens have the most deaths, what the sick hens have in common, and what the farm should do tomorrow. Epidemiology gives a method for these questions.

The simple definition says that epidemiology is the study of health-related events in populations. The full definition adds what the study looks at and what it is for.

### Population perspective

A clinical question concerns one animal. A population question concerns a group of animals and the pattern of health events in that group. The table compares the way a clinician and an epidemiologist work.

| Comparison | Clinician | Epidemiologist |
|-|-|-|
| Focus | Individuals | Populations |
| Objective | Curing the sick individual | Preventing and controlling disease in populations |
| Diagnosis | Based on anamnesis, physical examination, and diagnostic tests | Based on statistical analysis and epidemiological studies |
| Treatment | Giving medical or surgical treatment and care | Implementing animal health and public health interventions and policies |

The same health problem can raise both kinds of question. In a layer house with high mortality, the clinical question leads to the necropsy of a few hens, and the population question leads to a review of mortality by pen and by day. The veterinarian needs both: the clinical findings suggest a cause, and the population pattern shows how far the problem reaches and where to act.

## Characteristics

The full definition of epidemiology has six elements, and each one has a specific meaning in veterinary work.

| Element | Meaning in veterinary epidemiology |
|-|-|
| Study | A scientific and systematic activity based on data |
| Health-related states or events | Diseases, infections, deaths, injuries, production problems, welfare states, and food safety hazards |
| Specified populations | Defined groups of animals, such as herds, flocks, kennels, and village populations, and the animal products that come from them |
| Distribution | The frequency and pattern of health events by animal, place, and time |
| Determinants | Factors that change the frequency of health events: agent, host, environment, management, and economic and social factors |
| Application | Use of the results to prevent and control health problems, and to check whether the measures worked |

The elements follow a logical order. The study starts with a defined population and describes the distribution of health events in it. It then looks for determinants that explain the distribution, and it applies the findings to action.

The elements also point to the approaches of epidemiology. Distribution leads to descriptive epidemiology, determinants lead to analytical epidemiology, and application leads to choosing and checking control measures. [Approaches of epidemiology](approaches-of-epidemiology.md) describes these links.

Health-related states or events cover more than infectious disease. A veterinary epidemiologist also studies lameness in dairy cows, drops in egg production, welfare problems, and hazards in meat and eggs, and uses the same method for all of them.

## Application example

A production animal veterinarian graduated two years ago and now works as the technical veterinarian of a layer company in South Sulawesi. One morning a farm manager calls to report that 120 of 8,000 hens died in the past week in a flock that normally loses about 10 hens a week.

The veterinarian visits the farm the next day. She starts with the clinical question: she necropsies five hens and sends samples to a laboratory to find the cause of death.

She then turns to population questions and asks for the daily mortality record and the layout of the house. She finds that 90 of the 120 deaths came from the two pens next to the water inlet. Mortality in these pens began 4 days before her visit, and egg production there had already fallen in the week before the first deaths.

These facts change her decision. The laboratory result is still pending, but she knows that the problem is concentrated in two pens and began at a known time. She asks the manager to separate the pens, to record deaths daily by pen, and to check the water supply. In terms of the definition, she described the distribution by animal, place, and time, proposed determinants to test, and applied the first findings to action.

The laboratory later reports Salmonella in the hens. The population now includes the eggs that left the farm since the first deaths, so the company must find out which buyers received eggs from the two pens. The same method now covers the egg chain, and the study continues beyond the farm.

## Assumptions and limitations

The definition assumes that the population is clearly specified. When the group of animals is unclear, the counts are hard to interpret, and a rate needs a denominator, so the veterinarian must know how many animals were at risk.

Epidemiology describes and explains patterns in groups, and it does not replace clinical diagnosis. A population pattern can suggest a cause, but the cause of one animal's illness still needs examination and tests.

Students often assume that a determinant found in a population acts the same way in every animal. This assumption is not always correct.

Most epidemiological studies are observational. The investigator often cannot assign the exposure, so the evidence needs careful interpretation.

## Issues and debates

Textbooks word the definition in slightly different ways. Some describe the application as the prevention and control of health problems, and the wording of the other elements also differs between sources. The six elements above are common to most versions.
