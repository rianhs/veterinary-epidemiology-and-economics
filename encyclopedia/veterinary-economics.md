Veterinary economics is the application of economic principles to animal health. In veterinary practice, it helps people decide how to use scarce resources to protect animal health and the value of animals to owners and society. These resources include money, time, labour, vaccines, and veterinary services.

## Background

A veterinarian rarely works without limits. A farmer cannot treat every problem in the flock. A district animal health service has a fixed budget for the season, and a clinic has a limited number of staff hours. Every choice to use a resource for one purpose means that the resource is not available for another.

Economics studies how people choose under such limits. It gives veterinarians a way to compare what an action gains with what it costs, and to see who gains and who pays. This is useful because disease control rarely fails for lack of knowledge alone. Control also fails when a measure costs more than the owner can pay, or when benefit and cost fall on different people.

Epidemiology and economics answer different questions about the same problem. Epidemiology estimates what happens to disease with and without a measure. Economics estimates what that change is worth and what the measure costs. A decision about disease control needs both answers, because an effective measure can still be a poor use of resources.

## Characteristics

The field rests on five ideas. Each idea has a specific meaning in animal health.

| Idea | Meaning in veterinary economics |
|-|-|
| Scarcity and choice | Resources for animal health are limited, so every control measure competes with other uses |
| Opportunity cost | The cost of a choice is the value of the best alternative that is given up |
| Value of animal health | Disease reduces production, product quality, trade, welfare, and public health, and control can protect this value |
| Private and public benefits | Some benefits of disease control go to the owner, and others go to neighbours, consumers, or the whole country |
| Epidemiology and economics together | Epidemiology estimates what happens to disease, and economics estimates what it is worth and what it costs |

[Scarcity](../glossary.md) is the starting point. Money, vaccines, and veterinary services are not enough to meet all wants, so someone must choose. Economists describe the allocation of scarce resources to competing uses as the core of their discipline (Morris, 1999).

[Opportunity cost](../glossary.md) is the value of the best alternative use of a resource. It differs from the money that a person pays. A veterinarian who spends a day on one farm gives up the value of that day on another farm, even when no money changes hands.

Animal health has value because disease takes value away. Sick animals produce less meat, milk, or eggs, and they may produce products of lower quality. Disease can also close markets to trade, harm animal welfare, and affect human health when the disease is a zoonosis. Disease control protects these values, and the analysis must say which of them it counts.

The benefits of control reach different people. A farmer who vaccinates a flock protects the farm income, which is a private benefit. The same vaccination also lowers the chance that disease spreads to the neighbours. An [externality](../glossary.md) is an effect on people who do not take part in the decision. Freedom from an epidemic disease is close to a [public good](../glossary.md). Many people benefit at the same time, and it is hard to exclude anyone. Owners tend to invest too little in measures whose benefits go to others, and this is one reason why governments take part in disease control.

## Application example

A veterinarian graduated two years ago and now coordinates field programs for a district animal health service. The head of the service is the [decision maker](../glossary.md). The coordinator must advise on one use of a budget of IDR 60,000,000 for one season. These numbers are synthetic and are for teaching.

Two proposals compete for the budget. The first vaccinates village chickens against Newcastle disease, and most of its benefit goes to the families that keep the chickens. The second vaccinates dogs against rabies, and its benefit reaches all residents of the district, including people who own no dog. Each proposal costs about the same.

The coordinator first asks the epidemiological question: how many cases does each proposal prevent? She then asks the economic questions. What are the prevented cases worth, who receives the benefit, and who should pay? The opportunity cost of choosing the first proposal is the value of the second, and the reverse.

Some benefits do not have a market price. The value of a prevented human case of rabies and the welfare of the animals cannot be read from a price list. The coordinator therefore presents the numbers that she can estimate, states the values that she cannot price, and lets the head of the service decide. Economics structured the comparison, and the decision still belongs to the person who carries the responsibility.

## Assumptions and limitations

An economic analysis counts what can be measured and priced. Welfare, human health, and the livelihood of poor households carry value that a market price does not capture. A result in money can therefore leave out something important.

The result also depends on assumptions. Prices change, the perspective of the analysis changes who counts as a winner, and the epidemiological estimates behind the analysis carry their own uncertainty. A good analysis states these assumptions and shows how the result changes when they change.

Students often treat cost as the money that was spent. Opportunity cost is wider, because it includes the value of what was given up. Another common misunderstanding is that economics aims only at profit. In public programs, the aim is often to reach a health goal with the least use of resources.

## Issues and debates

Authors name and bound the field in different ways, and many call it animal health economics. This article uses one working definition for the course.

Analysts also differ on non-market values. Some use money-based methods to value welfare and public health inside the analysis. Others keep these values outside the calculation and present them next to the economic result, so that the decision maker weighs them. [verify]
