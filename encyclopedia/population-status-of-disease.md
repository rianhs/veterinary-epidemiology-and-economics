The population status of a disease is the state of that disease in a defined group of animals at a given time. It is described on a range from free of the disease to eliminated. In veterinary epidemiology, the group can be a herd, a flock, a district, or a country. The status points to the objective of the work.

## Background

A veterinarian can meet the same disease in very different situations. In one district the disease is absent. In a second district it appears in single cases. In a third district it is present in almost every village. The work that makes sense is different in each place, because each place has a different problem.

The literature has many words for these situations, such as sporadic, endemic, epidemic, and outbreak. Each word describes one part of the picture. The population status puts them in one order, from the absence of disease to its widest spread, and it links each position to an objective. This link helps a veterinarian decide what to do first.

## Characteristics

The table shows the nine statuses of the range, with the description and the typical objective of each.

| Population status | Description | Typical objective |
|-|-|-|
| Free | The disease is absent, with evidence | Prevent introduction, maintain freedom |
| Exposed, disease not established | The agent has entered, but the disease does not maintain itself | Prevent establishment |
| Sporadic | Single cases or small groups occur irregularly | Reduce, find the source |
| Endemic | The disease is constantly present at an expected level | Reduce, control, work toward elimination |
| Outbreak | Cases occur above the expected level in a limited place and time | Contain, find the source, stop spread |
| Epidemic | Cases occur clearly above the expected level in a wider area or population | Control spread, reduce impact |
| Pandemic | An epidemic spreads across several countries or continents | Control spread, coordinate across countries |
| Under control or close to elimination | Occurrence is reduced and kept low by measures | Keep the gains, complete elimination |
| Eliminated | The disease is removed from a defined area | Prevent reintroduction |

### Three groups of status

The nine statuses fall into three groups. In the first group, the disease is absent or has not become established: free, exposed with the disease not established, and eliminated. In the second group, the disease is present at an expected or low level: sporadic, endemic, and under control. In the third group, the disease occurs above the expected level: outbreak, epidemic, and pandemic.

The groups show that the words do not measure the same thing. Sporadic and endemic describe a pattern of occurrence. Outbreak and epidemic describe a change from the [baseline](../glossary.md), which is the expected level of disease. Free and eliminated describe the absence of disease and need evidence.

### The two objectives

The objective follows the status. When the disease is present in the population, the objective is to reduce the problem and, where feasible, to eliminate it. When the disease is absent, the objective is to keep it out, which means to prevent introduction and to maintain the absence.

Statuses in the first group lead to the second objective. Statuses in the second and third groups lead to the first objective. Import risk analysis serves both objectives, because it protects free populations and also protects control programs from new introductions.

### Status belongs to a population and a time

A status is always stated for a defined population and a period. The same disease can be endemic in one district, sporadic in a second district, and absent from a third. The status can also move in both directions. An outbreak can end and leave the disease sporadic, and a control program can bring an endemic disease to a low level.

The glossary of this course uses the term elimination for the removal of a disease from a defined area. Eradication is the removal of the agent from the whole world.

## Application example

A program coordinator at a provincial livestock service, two years after graduation, supervises the control of Newcastle disease in village chickens. She looks at three districts. The districts and the numbers are synthetic and are for teaching.

In District A, vaccination records and laboratory results show that the disease is present every year at a similar level. Its status is endemic, so her objective is to reduce the problem through vaccination and to work toward elimination. In District B, three villages reported cases in the last two years, with no link between them. The status is sporadic, so she asks the district officer to find the source of each case.

District C has had no case for five years, and its surveillance would detect the disease if it were present. The status is free, and the objective is to keep the disease out. She asks the officer to check poultry that enter the district from District A. Then District C reports 40 sick chickens in one village in one week. The status changes to outbreak, and the objective changes to containing the disease and finding its source.

## Assumptions and limitations

The boundaries between the statuses are not sharp. No fixed number separates sporadic from endemic, or outbreak from epidemic. The judgement depends on a baseline, and a baseline needs several years of reliable data.

A claim of freedom needs evidence. If a district has no reports, the disease can still be present because farmers do not report or no one tests. In this case, weak surveillance makes a disease look sporadic or absent. [Freedom from disease](../glossary.md) is shown by negative results from surveillance at a stated design prevalence and confidence.

The status belongs to a population, so one sick animal does not set it. A single suspected case in a free district first needs confirmation before anyone changes the status.

## Issues and debates

Sources use different words for the same situations. Some veterinary texts use enzootic for endemic and epizootic for epidemic (Thrusfield & Christley, 2018). [verify] This course uses endemic and epidemic, and students will meet the other words in older sources.

The range of nine statuses is a working choice of this course. Other sources define the terms one by one and do not place them on one range. International bodies also give an official status to a small number of diseases, with formal rules for recognition. That formal status is different from the range in this article. [citation needed]
