The approaches of epidemiology are the main ways in which epidemiologists work on a health problem in a population. In veterinary epidemiology, each approach follows from one element of the [definition of epidemiology](epidemiology.md) and answers one question about a population of animals.

## Background

The definition of epidemiology has several elements, and each element leads to a kind of work. Describing the distribution of health events leads to the descriptive approach. Looking for determinants leads to the analytical approach and, in some studies, to the experimental approach. Applying the results to control health problems leads to the work of choosing measures and checking whether they worked.

The approaches are not separate professions. One veterinarian uses all of them, often within one problem, and the result of one approach gives the starting point for the next. A veterinarian first describes a pattern and then asks why it exists. She estimates what could happen, chooses measures, and checks the results.

## Characteristics

The table links each approach to an element of the definition and to the question that the approach answers.

| Approach | Element of the definition | Question | Domain |
|-|-|-|-|
| Descriptive epidemiology | Distribution | What is happening? | D1 |
| Analytical epidemiology | Determinants | Why is it happening? | D2 |
| Experimental epidemiology | Determinants | Does an exposure that the investigator assigns change the outcome? | D2, advanced |
| Predictive epidemiology | Projection of what could happen | What could happen? | D3 |
| Interventional epidemiology | Application: prevention and control | What should be done? | D4 |
| Evaluation | Application: checking the results | Did it work? | D5 |

The column Domain shows the part of this course that treats the approach in depth. The labels predictive epidemiology and interventional epidemiology are working choices of the course (see Issues and debates).

### Descriptive epidemiology

The descriptive approach describes the distribution of health events by animal, place, and time. A table of deaths by pen, a map of cases by village, and an epidemic curve are descriptive work. The result is a pattern and a list of questions that the pattern leaves open.

### Analytical epidemiology

The analytical approach studies determinants by comparing groups. In an observational study, the investigator records exposures and does not assign them. An example is a comparison of parvovirus disease in vaccinated and unvaccinated puppies, using the records of a clinic.

### Experimental epidemiology

The experimental approach studies determinants or interventions in a trial. The investigator assigns the exposure, for example a vaccine, to animals in different groups and compares the outcomes. Designing and running a trial needs specialist training. Every veterinarian can still learn to recognize and appraise trials.

### Predictive epidemiology

The predictive approach projects what could happen with and without a measure. It uses risk assessment and models, such as a compartmental model that follows susceptible, infectious, and recovered animals. The result is a set of scenarios, and every scenario rests on stated assumptions.

### Interventional epidemiology

The interventional approach applies epidemiological evidence to choose measures that prevent and control health problems. It compares options against biological, regulatory, and financial limits, and it considers who benefits and who pays. [Veterinary economics](veterinary-economics.md) supplies the tools for this comparison.

### Evaluation

Evaluation checks whether a measure achieved its aim. It compares the disease or the cost before and after the measure, or between groups with and without the measure. A key question is whether the disease changed in the population or only the detection of the disease changed.

## Application example

A veterinarian two years after graduation works at an animal shelter in Makassar. Within five days, 14 of the 60 dogs develop vomiting and diarrhoea. These numbers are synthetic and are for teaching.

She starts with the descriptive approach. A line list and an epidemic curve show that most sick dogs live in one kennel block and that new cases appear every day. Then she turns to the analytical approach and compares the illness of vaccinated and unvaccinated dogs in the shelter records.

The predictive approach comes next. She estimates how many more dogs could fall ill in the coming week without action. She then estimates the number if the shelter isolates the sick dogs. She then compares options for the manager: isolation, vaccination of new arrivals, and a pause in intake. Each option has a cost in staff time and money, and each option affects the welfare of the dogs.

No trial is possible in the middle of an outbreak. She uses evidence from published trials on vaccine efficacy to judge the vaccination option. After three weeks, she uses evaluation to check the daily number of new cases. She also asks whether the decline is real or reflects fewer tests.

## Assumptions and limitations

The approaches do not form a strict sequence. A veterinarian may return to description after an analysis shows that a case definition was too narrow. A model may also point to data that the descriptive work must collect.

Each approach depends on the quality of the earlier work. An analysis of poor records gives weak comparisons, and a model that uses a wrong estimate of disease frequency gives misleading scenarios. Observational comparisons show association, and a causal conclusion needs additional appraisal.

Evidence from trials also supports decisions and evaluation. The table shows the main home of each approach, and each approach also appears in other places.

## Issues and debates

Textbooks group the work of epidemiology in different ways. A veterinary textbook can give surveys, observational studies, and clinical trials their own chapters. It can do the same for mathematical modelling, risk analysis, economics, and disease control (Thrusfield & Christley, 2018). Many sources describe descriptive, analytical, and experimental epidemiology as the main types. [verify]

The labels predictive epidemiology and interventional epidemiology are working choices of this course. Other sources use other names for the same work, so readers should expect different labels in other books.
